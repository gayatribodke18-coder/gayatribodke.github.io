01

The context

Mai sits inside a healthcare portfolio at AjnaLens. The hardware team had a smartband. The brief for design was the other half: the part that decides whether someone wears it in week six.

I joined at discovery, which meant the product definition was still moving. That was the useful part. Research could still change what we built rather than only how it looked.

02

The problem

Most health wearables ask for effort people do not have.

Trackers are good at measuring and bad at motivating. They pile on numbers, streaks and alerts until checking your health becomes another thing to feel guilty about. The brief was to design for the opposite: help someone build one or two small habits, then quietly get out of the way.

03

The approach

Research first, then design for the smallest nudge that works.

Listen

Interviews and diary studies on what makes people abandon a tracker, and what quietly keeps them coming back.

Frame

Turning those signals into behaviour principles: reduce guilt, celebrate small wins, make the next action obvious.

Prototype

Testing the on-wrist and in-app moments together, so a nudge feels like a nudge and never an alarm.

04

A decision I am working through

Onboarding that unfolds, not a form to fill.

Challenge

A product like this wants a lot of context to be useful. Asking for all of it up front, as one long health questionnaire, is friction at the exact moment someone is deciding whether to bother at all. It reads as a chore and pushes people to drop off before they ever feel a benefit.

Decision

Design onboarding as something that unfolds. Collect only what is needed to take the first step, then ask for each new detail in the moment it becomes relevant, always with a plain reason for why it helps. Setup becomes part of using the app, not a gate in front of it.

Why it matters

It lowers cognitive load on day one, builds trust by explaining each ask rather than demanding it, and lets the product personalise gradually as the relationship grows.
