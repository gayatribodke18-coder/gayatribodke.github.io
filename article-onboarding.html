When everything is important, the answer isn't "ask less"

A woman downloads an app to track her period. The product needs to understand far more than her cycle. Rethinking onboarding for Mai, one meaningful step at a time.

She downloaded an app to track her period

Her first thought is simple. When was my last period, how long is my cycle, maybe a few symptoms. She expects a calendar, a prediction, a reminder. That is what she came for.

Then the app starts asking. Not just about her period. Her current stage. Her lifestyle. Her medical history. Her family history. Her medications. Her allergies. Her reports.

What she came with

"I just want to track my period."

What it starts to feel like

"Why does this app need to know so much about me?"

That gap is where I started thinking differently about onboarding while working on Mai, a women's health app.

The problem wasn't that we were asking too much

My first instinct was the one I usually have with onboarding. Can we remove something? Fewer questions, less effort, faster access to the product.

But when I looked closely at what Mai needed, I realised something uncomfortable. In healthcare, a question is not optional just because it creates friction. Medical history gives context. Medications matter. Allergies matter. Family history matters. The stage of life she is in changes what is even relevant to her. A single missing piece means the product has less to work with.

If everything is important, how do we make it feel manageable?

Benchmarking

I went back to the apps women already use

Before changing anything, I studied onboarding across women's health apps. I wanted to see how existing products balanced collecting enough information against keeping the user moving.

How questions were structured
Which ones were made compulsory
How information was grouped
How progress was communicated
Whether they explained why they were asking
How sensitive questions were introduced

The friction was not coming from one difficult question. It came from the accumulation. Each question is reasonable on its own. Together they start to feel like a very long form.

What if we didn't ask less, but asked better?

Instead of one continuous questionnaire, I started treating onboarding as a journey where the woman's understanding grows alongside the information we collect. Break it into meaningful sections. Give each section a purpose. Explain that purpose. Show progress. Move forward. That became the foundation for progressive onboarding in Mai.

From one long questionnaire to a health profile

Rather than a single form, the information was divided into profiles. Each one represents another piece of understanding her.

Personal

The foundation of who she is.

Prakriti

Her individual constitution.

Stage

The stage she is in now, so the experience can stay relevant to it.

Medical

Past history, current health, family history, allergies, medications, reports.

SOS

What is needed when emergency support matters.

Sixth profile

The remaining profile area in the current structure.

The point was never six sections. It was changing how the woman experiences the information.

Every question had somewhere to go

Nothing was collected simply because we could collect it. Every question mapped to something in the product. That meant onboarding could introduce Mai's value one piece at a time, instead of explaining everything the app can do on the first screen.

She isn't only giving information. She is starting to understand what she gets in return.

"Why are you asking me this?"

Being asked about your medical history without context invites exactly that question. So the sections carry an explanation of how the information contributes to more relevant support. The explanation isn't extra content. It is part of the UX.

Then we made progress visible

Every long onboarding raises the same question: how much longer? The sections give the journey meaning, and the progress bar gives her orientation inside it. Together they take away the feeling that this has no end.

The interface had to answer questions too

Visuals, helper text, section introductions, progress indicators, clear CTAs. Each one answers something the woman is quietly asking while she fills the form. Healthcare information already feels serious. The interface didn't need to add more uncertainty. It needed to remove some.

What am I doing?
Why am I answering this?
How will this help me?
Where am I?
What happens next?
Who am I giving all this to?

Before asking her to share everything, we introduced Mai

Mai is introduced during onboarding, so the woman knows who is accompanying her through the health experience. It shifts the feeling from filling out information for an app towards beginning to tell someone about yourself.

That introduction is also part of revealing the product's value gradually. She doesn't have to understand everything Mai can do on the first screen. She discovers it as she builds her profile.

Trust starts before the dashboard

What we collect during onboarding is deeply personal, so how it is handled is part of the product story. Mai is being designed around an edge-device approach, where the data stays on the user's phone.

When a product asks someone to share sensitive health information, she should understand not only why we are asking, but what happens to what she gives us. The exact privacy wording follows the technical implementation, but from a UX perspective it belongs in the relationship we start during onboarding.

Not every woman wants health information in the same language

Mai is bilingual, English and Hindi. In onboarding that matters more than anywhere else. Medical terminology is hard, and a sensitive question gets harder when the wording is unfamiliar. Bilingual support isn't translating the interface. It is making sure the meaning survives.

Same information. Same intent. Better comprehension.

So did we actually make onboarding shorter?

Not really, and that is what makes the problem interesting. We didn't solve it by pretending healthcare needs less information. We changed how the information is experienced.

The complexity still exists. She just doesn't have to carry all of it at once.

The shift in my thinking

I used to look at onboarding and ask how many steps we could remove. Now I also ask how much complexity the user is carrying at each step.

Two experiences can collect exactly the same information and feel completely different. One feels like a medical form. The other can feel like a guided conversation. That difference is where UX comes in.

What designing for women's health taught me

Women's health isn't one problem. Not just periods, not just pregnancy, not just symptoms, not just a set of vitals. Her health experience changes across stages of her life, and the context around it matters. A product that wants to support her meaningfully often needs to understand more than what she came looking for.

She comes to solve one problem. The product needs the bigger picture. Onboarding has to bridge those two things.

When the information really is important, the better question isn't how to ask less. It is whether the reason for asking is clear, whether the effort is broken into meaningful pieces, whether she can see she is making progress, and whether the experience feels like something she is building rather than something she is completing.

One meaningful step at a time

Progressive onboarding wasn't a way to hide the complexity of women's healthcare. It was a way to make that complexity easier to navigate. A woman downloads an app thinking she just wants to track her period, and the product eventually needs to know much more. The UX challenge is making that transition feel natural.

Not

"Tell us everything before you can start."

But

"Let's get to know you, one meaningful step at a time."
